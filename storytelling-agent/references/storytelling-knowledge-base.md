# Storytelling Knowledge Base

This is the normalized knowledge layer distilled from the attached materials. It is not a transcript dump. Use it to reason about narrative design.

## Ontology

### Premise

- **Idea / Concept:** seed or hook.
- **Premise:** surface promise; what the audience thinks the story is about.
- **Logline:** concise expression of protagonist + goal + obstacle + stakes.
- **Dramatic Question:** question that keeps the audience watching.
- **Core:** deeper human/emotional/philosophical engine beneath the premise.
- **Representation:** fictional/audiovisual vessel chosen to express the core.

Key relation: `premise enables core` is stronger than `core pasted onto premise`.

### Character

- Want: external pursuit.
- Need: transformation, recognition, or missing inner truth.
- Fear: avoided consequence.
- Flaw/belief: limiting operating logic.
- Contradiction: humanizing tension.
- Motivation: why action matters.
- Backstory: relevant pressure, not biography dump.
- Arc: transformation or revelation through choices.
- Relationships: pressure systems that expose character.

### Conflict

- Internal: self vs self.
- Interpersonal: person vs person.
- Environmental: person vs world/nature/system.
- Societal: person vs institution/culture/class/order.
- Philosophical: values/worldviews in conflict.

### Stakes

- Physical, emotional, relational, social, moral, existential.
- Stakes are not just “what can be lost”; they are why choices matter.
- Strong stakes usually combine consequence + specificity + escalation.

### Structure

- Setup.
- Inciting incident.
- Progressive complications.
- Turning points / reversals.
- Midpoint or major reorientation.
- Crisis: decision under maximum pressure.
- Climax: action/result of the decisive choice.
- Resolution: emotional and causal settling.

No structure is universal. Select structure based on problem, format, and intended experience.

### Scene

- Objective.
- Obstacle.
- Conflict.
- Beat.
- Turn.
- Value change.
- Reveal.
- Escalation.
- Consequence.

A scene should change the story state: value, information, relationship, objective, power, danger, or emotional condition.

### Information

- Mystery: audience lacks information and wants answer.
- Suspense: audience anticipates outcome, often with danger/urgency.
- Dramatic irony: audience knows more than character.
- Withholding: delaying information to create desire.
- Foreshadowing: planting future meaning.
- Setup/payoff: promise and fulfillment.
- Reveal: new information changes interpretation.

### Emotion

- Anticipation, tension, surprise, empathy, dread, relief, grief, wonder, catharsis.
- Track emotional movement over time, not just plot events.
- Vonnegut-style story shapes are useful for emotional trajectory, not full design.

### Theme

- Central question.
- Values in conflict.
- Worldview.
- Moral dilemma.
- Transformation.
- Controlling idea / meaning created by the final value change.

Theme should be dramatized through choices and consequences, not merely stated.

### Visual Storytelling

- Visual metaphor.
- Blocking.
- Composition.
- Contrast.
- Symbolic objects.
- Environmental storytelling.
- Non-verbal behavior.
- Sound/silence/music.
- Montage and rhythm.

Visual storytelling is not “pretty images.” It is meaning carried by visible/audible action.

### Rhythm

- Pacing.
- Escalation.
- Compression.
- Breathing room.
- Repetition with variation.
- Contrast.
- Information timing.

### Ending

- Payoff.
- Resolution.
- Reversal.
- Transformation/revelation.
- Emotional landing.
- Final image.
- Answer to the dramatic question.

## Principles

### P01 — Principles over rules

- Description: Use storytelling principles as tools, not commandments.
- Sources: McKee; user instruction.
- Application: Diagnose first; choose tool second.
- Limitation: Avoid “always” and “never” unless the user asks for a hard constraint.

### P02 — Premise is not the story

- Description: The premise is the facade; the core is what the story is really exploring.
- Sources: Lofi Cinema masterclass.
- Application: For every premise, ask what human question or emotional engine lives underneath.
- Risk: If premise and story are identical, the result may feel thin or generic.

### P03 — Core must grow organically from premise

- Description: The emotional/thematic engine should feel enabled by the premise, not pasted onto it.
- Sources: Lofi Cinema, especially Gravity/In Time/Passengers discussion.
- Application: Test whether the core could only emerge strongly from this premise or whether it is an interchangeable love/family/redemption engine.

### P04 — Originality comes from transformed core, not surface novelty

- Description: A familiar premise can feel original if its core and representation transform it; a clever premise can feel generic if the core is safe.
- Sources: Lofi Cinema cliché/convention chapters; Schrader authorial metaphor; Martin emotional truth.
- Application: When idea feels cliché, do not discard first; search for a different core/representation relationship.

### P05 — Emotional truth grounds invention

- Description: “Write what you know” means emotional truth rather than literal experience.
- Sources: George R. R. Martin; also resonant with Docter/Erickson/Lofi.
- Application: Fantasy, sci-fi, animation, or surreal premises should still express recognizable desire, fear, shame, love, grief, ambition, loneliness, etc.

### P06 — Character is revealed by pressured choice

- Description: What a character chooses under pressure reveals who they are.
- Sources: McKee.
- Application: If character feels flat, create decisions with cost and consequence.

### P07 — Scenes need value change

- Description: A scene should turn from one value state to another: hope→fear, trust→doubt, safety→danger, ignorance→knowledge, power→weakness.
- Sources: McKee scene design.
- Application: Cut/merge/rework scenes with no change.

### P08 — Causality beats chronology

- Description: Strong stories progress through because/therefore, not merely and-then.
- Sources: McKee structure logic; user instruction.
- Application: Test every major beat for causal consequence.

### P09 — Opposition reveals design

- Description: The antagonistic force should be strong enough to expose character, deepen theme, and escalate choices.
- Sources: McKee principle of antagonism.
- Application: Weak conflict often means weak opposition, unclear desire, or insufficient consequence.

### P10 — Exposition should be dramatized

- Description: Information is best released under pressure, through action, conflict, discovery, or visual evidence.
- Sources: McKee exposition; Schrader cinematic embodiment; user visual priority.
- Application: Convert explanation into behavior, object, conflict, image, or consequence.

### P11 — Humor can come from character under pressure

- Description: Comedy is stronger when it emerges from characters trying to survive or make life inside constraints.
- Sources: Dan Erickson on *Severance*.
- Application: In dark premises, humor can deepen attachment rather than undercut stakes.

### P12 — Simple starting ideas are workable

- Description: Demanding a perfect original premise at the ideation stage creates paralysis. Work the idea.
- Sources: Lofi Cinema P.S.; Vonnegut simplicity.
- Application: Start with the simple idea, then develop core, contrast, pressure, and representation.

## Techniques

- **Setup and payoff:** plant expectation or information, then fulfill or transform it.
- **Dramatic irony:** let the audience know something the character does not.
- **Withholding information:** delay answers to create desire or tension.
- **Reveal:** new information changes what the audience thinks the story is about.
- **Reversal:** expected result flips into a different value/outcome.
- **False victory / false defeat:** temporary result that misleads character/audience and sets up deeper turn.
- **Ticking clock:** time pressure intensifies choices.
- **Escalation:** each attempt makes the situation harder, clearer, or more consequential.
- **Rule of three:** establish pattern, vary it, then complete or break it.
- **Visual metaphor:** image/object/action carries abstract meaning.
- **Compression:** preserve core causal/emotional spine while removing redundant beats.
- **Core shift:** keep familiar premise but change what the story is actually about.
- **Representation shift:** express familiar core through an unexpected but organic vessel.
- **Emotional graphing:** track rise/fall of character fortune or emotional state over time.
- **Cinematic embodiment:** turn theme/exposition into image, action, rhythm, or formal constraint.

## Structures

Treat these as selectable tools:

- **Three Act Structure:** useful for feature-length setup/confrontation/resolution problems; avoid as default template.
- **Sequence Approach:** useful when second act lacks progression; divide into smaller goal/reversal units.
- **McKee value-change design:** useful for scenes and acts where nothing changes.
- **Crisis → Climax → Resolution:** useful for weak endings and unearned climaxes.
- **Story shape / emotional curve:** useful for tonal trajectory and audience experience.
- **Premise → Core → Representation workflow:** useful for idea development and originality.
- **Short-form compression spine:** hook → conflict/turn → payoff/final image; useful for ads/social/shorts.
- **Mystery engine:** question → clues → misdirection → reveal → recontextualization; useful for series/high-concept.
- **No-dialogue visual spine:** objective → obstacle → visible attempts → visual turn → final image.

## Story Patterns

### Familiar premise, new core

- Mechanism: use a recognizable setup but attach a less expected human/emotional question.
- Effect: audience has quick access but discovers depth.
- Sources: Lofi Cinema cliché/convention.
- Risk: if core is not organic, it feels pasted on.

### Personal experience, genre metaphor

- Mechanism: begin with lived feeling, find speculative/visual representation.
- Effect: genre concept gains emotional truth.
- Sources: Martin, Erickson, Docter, Lofi.
- Risk: metaphor too obvious or too distant.

### Escape/survival as inner rebirth

- Mechanism: external survival pressure represents internal decision to live/change.
- Sources: Lofi discussion of *Gravity*.
- Effect: physical stakes and emotional stakes reinforce each other.

### Trapped community comedy

- Mechanism: characters trapped in oppressive conditions use humor to create life.
- Sources: Erickson on *Severance*.
- Effect: comedy humanizes darkness.

### Value-pressure scene

- Mechanism: scene starts in one value, conflict applies pressure, scene turns into opposite/altered value.
- Sources: McKee.
- Effect: every scene advances story state.

### Final image as thematic proof

- Mechanism: ending lands as image/action that proves transformation or irony.
- Sources: McKee resolution; visual storytelling synthesis.
- Effect: audience feels conclusion rather than just understands it.

## Contradictions / Strategy Forks

### Start with conflict vs start with normality

- Strategy A: Start with conflict for immediate momentum.
- Strategy B: Start with normality to create contrast before disruption.
- Use A when short format, genre hook, urgency, or attention economy matters.
- Use B when emotional contrast, world texture, or transformation baseline matters.

### Structure vs discovery

- McKee-style structure helps diagnose and design; Lofi emphasizes working the idea/core before imposing structure.
- Use structure after identifying the problem. Do not let structure replace discovery.

### Clever premise vs personal truth

- Clever premise can create access but risks hollow novelty.
- Personal truth can create depth but risks obvious metaphor.
- Best path often connects both: accessible premise + authentic core + organic representation.

### Simplicity vs sophistication

- Vonnegut/Lofi support simple starting shapes; Schrader supports formal/personal intensity.
- Choose simplicity when clarity/emotion is the priority; choose formal complexity when the project’s intended experience benefits from it.

## Questions Library

### Premise/Core

- What does the audience think this story is about?
- What is it actually about underneath?
- Does the core grow naturally from the premise?
- If the premise vanished, would the emotional story still be generic?
- What is the least obvious but still organic core?

### Character

- What does the protagonist want right now?
- Why can’t they walk away?
- What do they believe at the start?
- What choice would cost them something real?
- What contradiction makes them human?

### Conflict/Stakes

- What blocks the protagonist externally?
- What blocks them internally?
- What gets worse if they fail?
- What becomes harder after each attempt?
- Are stakes physical, emotional, relational, moral, or existential?

### Scene

- What changes between the first and last beat?
- What information is revealed or withheld?
- What is the turn?
- Which next scene happens because of this one?

### Ending

- What question did the beginning ask?
- Does the climax answer that exact question?
- What setup is paid off?
- What final image proves the change?
- Is the ending inevitable retrospectively but surprising in the moment?

### Visual Storytelling

- How can this be shown without explaining?
- What object, gesture, or environment can carry the information?
- What would the audience understand with sound muted?
- Where can silence do more than dialogue?

## Example Mapping

- *Gravity*: premise/representation of survival and escape; core can become rebirth/will to live when organic to premise. Source: Lofi Cinema.
- *In Bruges*: personal experience of beauty/boredom becomes premise/core contrast. Source: Lofi Cinema.
- *Inside Out*: emotional/psychological concept made visible through animation; source: Lofi and Pete Docter material.
- *Severance*: high-concept workplace split grounded in work-life alienation and character-based comedy. Source: Dan Erickson.
- *The Last of Us* vs *Pluribus*: similar broad zombie/infection territory can differ by core question and audience relationship to transformation. Source: Lofi Cinema.
- *Little Miss Sunshine*: familiar underdog/family premise can become fresh through core and payoff. Source: Lofi Cinema.

## Source / Principle / Inference Distinction

When asked for provenance:

- **SOURCE KNOWLEDGE:** explicitly appears in source catalog/material.
- **GENERAL STORYTELLING PRINCIPLE:** normalized synthesis across sources.
- **AGENT INFERENCE:** application to the user’s specific story.

Example:

```text
SOURCE KNOWLEDGE: Lofi Cinema distinguishes premise, core, and representation.
GENERAL PRINCIPLE: A premise needs an organic deeper engine.
AGENT INFERENCE: In your caveman/robot idea, the most promising core may be fear→curiosity→connection rather than just primitive-vs-futuristic comedy.
```
